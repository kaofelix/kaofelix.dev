I grew up in Porto Alegre, the capital of Brazil's southmost state, Rio Grande do Sul. On my childhood, there was a game which was quite popular across generations. Me and other kids my age loved it, but also my uncles, and even my grandpa. This was "[Button Football](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Button_football)".

I came across quite a few table football variations growing up, but Button Football was the only one that I took seriously as a game. There were fancier looking versions with actual football player shaped plastic pieces with a leg that could be moved like a little lever to make it kick the ball, but those were kind of impractical and too loose to work well as a competitive game.

In Button Football, your players are colorful acrylic discs. Other than an initial arrangement of your team in each kick off and a few other exceptional situations, you don't touch your players with your hands. You use a "palheta" (palette), a thin plastic disc that you press over your players with a slight tilt to make them propel forward. It might sound weird, but it's very tactile and intuitive once you see how it's done.

As I started to do research for this post, I found out there are quite a few ruleset variants which include also different field dimensions, player contours, goalie and ball shapes. Some I knew, others I have never seen in my life. When I was very little, I had a bunch of really cheap plastic ones which, while they had the basic mechanics and gameplay, the feel was not as great

<PostImage src={plasticButtons} alt="Plastic button football pieces" caption="Not quite the ones I had, but close enough" />

As I grew up, I can't remember when, but I think I owe mostly to my uncles, to introduce the "real" button football to me.

A big part of my relationship to the hobby revolved around this [little shop](https://bazarmimo.com.br), which is just a few blocks away from the apartment where I grew up. Looking on Google Maps today, seems like they are still going.

<PostImage src={theShop} alt="The little shop that sold button football supplies" />

I still remember getting my first "pro" set from there that came in a wooden case with the slots to fit the full team, the goalie and some extras like the palette, ball and the goal itself.

They sold the buttons that were "proper", let's say. Those were heavier, perfectly round from being made in a lathe out of a slab of acrylic resin (or so I think, I'm not so knowledgeable about materials like this, we just called it "acrílico" in Portuguese at the time).

They slid across the table in a much more controlled and consistent way. The *feel* was so much better. Those, combined with a proper table, a ball and a set of rules make for a really great and well balanced competitive game.

There was a collectable element to it as well as there were so many different looks and sizes. Some had the "pearly" exterior that had that underlying sheen like metallic car paint. They were just delighful to look at and handle, which I think also adds to the overall good feel of the game.

<PostImage src={acrylicButtons} alt="Acrylic button football pieces" />

And, with the proper pieces, you get the refined skill based gameplay that is actually compelling to learn. You're not busy blaming your shoddy plastic pieces for the wildly random results of each shot. You have enough control and precision that you *should* at least blame your lack of skill (well, *of course* you would still blame the pieces or the table or whatever). It's like the difference between playing pool with empty plastic gacha machine balls and a long wobbly rubber tube as the cue.

## Button Football, The Game

Given you have this exquisitely made resin pieces, what is missing is just a ruleset. As I mentioned before, there were lots of those, but as a kid I only really cared about two: Toque Toque, and 1-toque.

### Toque-Toque

Toque Toque (pronounced tock-tock) was the more informal ruleset. This is the one that I would play at home with my friends or my uncles. I had a small table and a varied set of different buttons. Not only from the shop I mentioned before, but also some that my uncles made themselves. Those were kind of legendary, they were named after famous football players at the time and when my uncles played with those it's like they had superpowers, at least in my child mind.

Since they were so heterogeneous, we would often assemble our team taking turns to pick one each until forming a full team.

So that meant that if it's your turn, you would play until:

1. The ball goes out after touching one of your buttons last, which is an offside
2. You bump into an opponent's button before touching the ball, which is a foul and they get a free shot at the place it happened
3. You score a goal, which means it's your opponent's turn to kick off
4. You miss the ball or the ball touches an opponent button last, which means your opponent can choose a button to use subject to these same rules

Goal shoots had to be declared, to give your opponent the chance to position their goalie, which was typically a cuboid made from the same material with a small diagonal cut on one of the edges that would be against the field, forming a little gap that could "catch" the "ball".

The ball, which was a much smaller plastic disc, had two sides: one flat and one concave. You could choose to flip it around before a shot. When the concave side faced the floor, the edge of the button would hit and make it lift off, so it was the choice for aerial shots that could go over the oponent's goalie.

I don't remember all the details and nuance of different specific situations and also it could vary a lot depending on "house rules" or us wanting to tweak or fighting over certain things. But this made for a very dynamic game, with higher scores and a more aggressive play style.

### 1-Toque

Literally meaning "one touch", this was basically turn-based table football. Players would alternate their plays, no matter if you had the chance to touch the ball or not.

The 1-toque ruleset was more official. This one was regulated and there were competitions organised around it. The ball, the players, goalies, etc, were all regulated as well, so it was a properly balanced competitive game. If you're curious, I found a <a href="/regra-gaucha.pdf" download>rule book</a> online. It's in Portuguese, but you can still probably pass it through some machine translation and get a good sense of it.

This ruleset made for games that were a lot more strategic in nature and the increase in table size meant that skill and movement precision had to be taken into account a lot more. Because of that, the scores would often look closer to a real football match. Also, since ball possession was not a thing, movement was not only towards the ball, but also for player positioning.

Unfortunately, I played with this ruleset very little. You needed a pro sized table, which was very large and expensive. So I probalby have played at most once or twice. The one I remember is when I joined a tournament. I didn't win any matches, so I didn't go very far. Although I was pretty good at the toque-toque one at home, 1-toque was a whole different skillset. However, I became forever fascinated by it. Even though I couldn't articulate it at the time, I guess I already felt that there was a much higher skill ceiling, which also means space for learning.

That feeling and yearning for having another opportunity to play this game again is one of the reasons that compelled me to write this post.

## Button Football: the Video Game

Before I started writing it though, I started daydreaming about building a digital version of it. I could see so clearly in my mind the final product: customizable buttons, cute low-poly graphics, etc. As for the gameplay, imagine turn based tactical football, but with pool physics. I think it's a cool competitive idea at least.

When I think about it, would be hard to capture the feel of the game with a realistic physics simulation probably. The nuance of moving the palette over the button is so tactile that it's hard to emulate with controls. So probably the way to go would be something a bit more arcadey that feels better as a video game. 

When I first got this wave of excitement, I started building a little 2D prototype with Godot and, thanks to the 2D physics engine and the Node system, I had a working prototype in a matter of a few hours. I often have many ideas all the time, but lack the persistence to follow through with many of them. This used to bring me down a lot, but later in live I think it's ok and don't let this hold me back from exploring and toying with an idea, as short lived as it might be.

So, while building my full idea of what Button Football could be as a video game would be a big one, I thought I could just do this prototype as a little toy. I'm calling it a toy and not a game, since it has no rules baked in. But I figure if I'm talking about a game, a small interactive representation of it is probably a lot more effective to convey the idea than just explaining. So I embeded it in this post.

To play, just hover your mouse over any of the buttons, click and pull to control the strength, release to sling the button in the opposite direction. Probably feels more intuitive than describing it, so give it a try!
